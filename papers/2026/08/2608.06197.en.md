## Conclusion summary

- EnvACE alternates a shared policy between actor and environment roles: it emits a tool call, predicts the resulting response, and conditions subsequent actions on that imagined observation. Both roles share parameters and are jointly optimized from terminal task reward.
- EnvACE-8B averages 32.91% across BFCL-v4, tau-squared-Bench, and VitaBench, exceeding environment-scaling baselines with complete results. On FinMCP-Bench, its tool F1 is 46.78%, 3.10 points above EnvScaler-8B.
- On tau-squared-Bench, standard GRPO scores 31.2%, separate actor and rehearsal policies score 35.5%, and shared-policy EnvACE scores 36.7%. The extra 1.2 points support, but do not fully prove, the internalization mechanism.
- Two private parallel rehearsals before execution raise the combined test-time score from 36.7% to 40.9%. Performance falls when the budget reaches three rehearsals, so added inference is not monotonically useful.
- Score: 90/100. The method reduces dependence on online executable environments during agent RL. Evaluation stops at 8B, training uses 16 NVIDIA H20 GPUs, test-time scaling is single-run, and total training time, tokens, energy, and judge cost are not reported.

## Research question and background

Long-horizon tool-agent RL commonly needs real APIs, resettable environments, or programmatic simulators. Real systems are expensive and side-effectful; synthesized environments require state machines and verifiers; separate LLM simulators may be ungrounded. EnvACE asks whether environment-response generation can become a role of the policy itself, allowing the model to learn action-outcome relationships without online environment interaction during training and to rehearse privately before real execution.

The method does not eliminate environment knowledge. It still relies on CM2 data, tool schemas, task-success rewards, and an LLM judge. Its narrower claim is that rollout state transitions can be generated within the policy rather than obtained from an external environment or simulator.

## Method

The shared policy alternates two output types. The actor conditions on dialogue and history to generate a tool call. The rehearsal role reads the tool allow-list, JSON schema, prior calls, history, and current action, then predicts an execution result. Later actions consume that prediction as an observation. Both roles inherit the trajectory reward, but role-wise GRPO computes separate group baselines before updating shared parameters.

At test time, parallel rehearsal generates independent imagined trajectories; sequential rehearsal lets later attempts revise earlier ones. The system compresses trajectories and self-evaluations into rehearsal memory and performs one committed execution against the external system. Figure 1 on paper page 2 contrasts real rollouts, external simulation, and shared-policy rehearsal. Figures 7-8 on pages 14-15 illustrate parameter repair and avoidance of a forbidden write. No screenshots are copied here because the tables and controlled comparisons carry the main evidence.

## Experiments and evidence

The main backbone is Qwen3-8B trained on CM2 data for 470 steps with learning rate `1e-6`, batch size 16, four rollouts per prompt, 64 instances per step, 12,000 input and 8,000 response tokens, and at most 30 interaction turns. Qwen3-30B-A3B serves as the training judge. Training uses 16 NVIDIA H20 GPUs. Non-test-time-scaling results are Avg@4 over four independent runs; test-time-scaling results are single-run because of cost.

EnvACE-8B scores 46.04% on BFCL-v4, 36.7% on tau-squared-Bench, and 16.0% on VitaBench, for a 32.91% arithmetic mean. This is 0.99 points above EnvScaler-8B and 0.37 above AWM-14B. These margins are small and no uncertainty intervals are reported, so the evidence supports competitive overall performance rather than universal dominance.

On FinMCP-Bench, EnvACE records 41.23% tool recall, 54.04% precision, and 46.78% F1. Its F1 exceeds EnvScaler-8B by 3.10 points, trading recall for the highest precision.

The tau-squared-Bench ablation gives 30.0% for base Qwen3, 31.2% for standard GRPO, 35.5% for separate role policies, and 36.7% for shared EnvACE. The experiment supports both response generation and parameter sharing, but does not independently measure calibration between rehearsed and real transitions.

Without rehearsal, the mean of tau-squared-Bench and BFCL Multi-Turn is 36.7%. Two parallel EnvACE rehearsals reach 40.9%; two sequential rehearsals reach 38.5%. Rehearsing with the base model gives only 36.8% and 34.9%. A third rehearsal lowers performance, which the authors attribute to longer inputs approaching the effective context limit.

## Limitations and risks

The authors explicitly limit the study to models no larger than 8B and mostly tool-interactive tasks. Test-time scaling is expensive and reported from one run.

The environment is removed only from rollout transitions, not from schemas, training data, reward verification, or the judge. A systematically wrong rehearsal model can reinforce an incorrect world model. The case studies show safe behavior, but the paper does not separately report rehearsal calibration, accumulated simulation error, or safety failure rates.

Main-result leads of 0.37-0.99 points have no confidence intervals or significance tests. The paper omits total GPU hours, generated tokens, judge cost, energy, and end-to-end inference latency. Private rehearsal increases compute and context pressure even when it reduces external calls.

## Reproduction and application

The public repository contains implementation, launchers, pinned dependencies, Docker assets, tests, and an Apache-2.0 license. The README specifies Python 3.12, PyTorch 2.6.0, SGLang 0.4.6.post5, Ray 2.49.2, and a reference run using two eight-GPU nodes. Model weights were still marked as forthcoming at collection time; full reproduction also needs CM2 data and the Qwen3-30B-A3B judge.

The most plausible deployment pattern is pre-execution rehearsal for high-impact tools: propose a call, predict its effect, check constraints, and submit only the final action. Production systems should continuously compare imagined and real responses, cap rehearsal budgets, and retain real verifiers for payments, writes, and permission changes. A generative world model is not a security boundary.

## Related work

- [Simulator](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.01824) uses a separate reasoning model to simulate environments; EnvACE folds simulation into the actor and updates both roles jointly.
- [EnvScaler](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05808) programmatically synthesizes interactive environments; EnvACE targets the cost of constructing them.
- [ScaleEnv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06820) scales environments, tasks, and verifiers from scratch; EnvACE moves scaling into policy rehearsal.
- [Agent World Model](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10090) builds code-driven synthetic worlds; EnvACE shares the world model's weights with the actor.
- [World Modelling Improves Language Model Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.02918) establishes the value of world modeling; EnvACE adds role-wise GRPO for joint action and response learning.
