## Conclusion summary

- OmniDR-Bench turns “research after watching a video” into an artifact-level task: an agent must organize work around audiovisual evidence, gather external text, image, and video evidence, and deliver an interactive report with timestamp links and claim-level citations.
- The benchmark contains 50 bilingual tasks, 19 subcategories, 3,102 audited facts, and 6,414 atomic criteria. The best of 18 model–harness configurations scores 63.3/100; the runner-up scores 62.0, and the paired confidence interval for their gap includes zero.
- The main shared weakness is not layout. Presentation averages 81.8, while content and citation average 45.4 and 47.8. External-evidence, cross-source verification, and multi-fact composition pass rates are only 8.7%, 8.8%, and 5.0%.
- Automatic scoring has initial human support: machine–human atomic agreement is 91.0% on 30 reports, and the Spearman correlation with holistic ratings is 0.64. However, all semantic criteria rely on one commercial multimodal judge, and absolute calibration-reference scores are not released.
- Evaluation code and the 50-task JSONL are public. At a fixed commit, 29 tests and 40 subtests pass locally. Full reproduction still requires obtaining 42 source videos, commercial model access, a browser environment, and substantial token and wall-time budgets.

## Research question and background

Existing video-agent evaluations usually stop at long-video understanding, retrieval, or short answers. Text deep-research benchmarks do not require work to remain organized around one video's audiovisual and temporal evidence. Real requests often ask whether a demonstrated procedure is reliable, how a video claim compares with current standards, or whether a visible parameter can be reproduced. Such requests cannot be solved by video summarization or generic web research alone.

The paper defines Video-Centered Deep Research Report Generation: input is a source video and a research request; output is an interactive HTML report that grounds speech, visuals, actions, and timestamps while connecting conclusions to external multimodal sources.

## Method

OmniDR-Bench uses a three-level Omni Atomic Rubric Tree. The top level contains content, citation, and presentation. The middle level has 23 shared directions. The leaves are task-specific binary criteria. A multimodal model judges semantic and visual criteria, while deterministic programs and browser probes check structure and interaction. Gates zero failed directions, and saturation prevents high-volume directions from dominating the score.

Audited Rubric Construction gathers evidence before creating criteria. The video branch cannot browse the web, and the web branches cannot view the source video. They independently record claims, timestamps, sources, and uncertainty before reconciling support, conflict, update, and application relationships. Evaluated reports never enter evidence collection or criterion generation. Static checks, behavioral calibration, repeat scoring, and human review reduce 82 candidate packages to 60 and then to the released 50.

The reference workflow performs audiovisual parsing, anchor selection, multimodal retrieval, synthesis, report generation, and citation/interface checks. Other systems are not required to follow it; only the delivered report is scored.

## Experiments and evidence

Across 900 runs, 835 reports are evaluable. Claude Opus 5 under Claude Code scores 63.3 with 96.0% completion; GLM-5.3 scores 62.0 with 100% completion. The paired 95% interval for their 1.3-point difference is [-4.0, 5.6], so the evidence supports a close top tier rather than a stable winner.

Across configurations, presentation averages 81.8, content 45.4, and citation 47.8. Direct-video facts pass at 79.9%, while external evidence, cross-source verification, and multi-fact composition pass at 8.7%, 8.8%, and 5.0%. Video faithfulness reaches 92.4%, but logical consistency and claim support reach only 21.2% and 15.9%. Some systems satisfy 98.0% of source-count criteria while supporting only 23.9% of claims.

On 15 matched tasks, adding full video-research orchestration raises the score from 46.6 to 53.9, improves 12 tasks, and has a paired 95% interval of [3.4, 11.1]. Citation gains 20.7 points. Median steps rise from 103 to 167, wall time from 0.7 to 1.6 hours, and tokens from 2.3M to 4.7M. This is a deployed quality–cost comparison, not iso-compute efficiency.

Three human raters produce 93.0% unanimous atomic decisions on 30 reports; machine–human majority agreement is 91.0%. Omni-ART correlates with holistic human ratings at Spearman 0.64. A final audit marks over 94% of 1,000 sampled fact/criterion items correct.

## Limitations and risks

The benchmark has only 50 tasks, and some of its 19 subcategories contain one to five tasks. Language and user-demand coverage are limited. A shared report interface may favor the reference skills, and complete attempts can require hundreds of steps.

All semantic criteria use one commercial multimodal judge, creating possible family-, language-, and style-dependent bias. Human validation covers 30 reports from only two model families, and 0.64 is moderate rather than near-perfect correlation. Pith further notes that the paper does not publish absolute R1/R2/R3 calibration scores or their L1–L3 pass rates, leaving criterion attainability less transparent than the central bottleneck claim requires.

The dataset releases queries, video URLs, and rubrics, but not video/audio files. Source videos may change, disappear, or be region restricted. CC BY-NC 4.0 covers dataset text and arrangement, not third-party media. Results also depend on commercial model versions and live-web state.

## Reproduction and application

The MIT-licensed evaluation repository was fixed at `573b7ecaf576c343ca739d7b232b7de96562cb48`. Locally, `python3 -m pytest -q` reports 29 passed and 40 subtests passed; `python3 -m compileall` passes. The public JSONL contains 50 rows, 50 unique task IDs, and 6,414 atomic criteria.

Playwright, source videos, a commercial judge, and the 900-run evaluation were not executed. The local checks establish the public evaluator's basic contracts and data shape, not independent reproduction of leaderboard scores.

The most useful engineering transfer is artifact-level acceptance: build a fact library independently, then score content coverage, cross-source verification, claim support, citation closure, and rendered behavior separately. Search counts, source counts, or page completion alone miss the “polished artifact without integrated evidence” failure.

## Research judgment

- **Priority: high.** Relevant to deep-research agents, multimodal reports, rubrics, citation audits, and artifact-level acceptance.
- **Confidence: medium-high.** Independent evidence construction, human audits, task bootstraps, paired intervals, and public code support the main result; a single judge, limited task coverage, and calibration transparency constrain generalization.
- **Audience:** teams that need to verify whether research evidence actually reaches a delivered artifact. This is not a generic video-QA leaderboard and does not establish a stable model winner.
- **Watch next:** full third-party reproduction, source-video durability, released calibration details, multi-judge and broader human validation, iso-token ablations, and whether new systems improve L1–L3 evidence integration rather than only report completion.
