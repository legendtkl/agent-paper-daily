## Conclusion summary

- Dynamic concurrency is not the default accelerator. In 2,124 executions of 354 tasks, 3 Agents, both concurrent and serial modes, concurrency generally increases tokens and runtime, and significantly reduces success rates on several software engineering benchmarks.
- The benefits of concurrency vary with the parallelizable structure of the task. The average test pass rate of the three types of Agents on LoopsBench increased, while the average test pass rate on RepoZero dropped by 8.29 percentage points; "multiple sub-Agents" cannot be regarded as a fixed strategy unrelated to the task.
- The most important output of the paper is not just the ranking list, but the attribution of failures to concurrency mechanisms such as shared write coverage, dependency misordering, premature convergence of the main agent, and unintegrated sub-agent results.
- The open artifact covers all 2,124 tracks and includes task-by-task evaluation and some manual annotations. However, the warehouse exceeds 2 GB and the root directory lacks a clear aggregation entry. This time, only the samples and frozen results are verified, and there is no independent re-run of the business model experiment.

## Research question and background

Long-range encoding agents can dynamically derive child agents. Intuitively, multi-path exploration can shorten wall clock time and increase coverage; but real coding tasks include shared files, serial dependencies, unified testing and final integration, and concurrency will also introduce coordination costs. The paper wants to answer three questions: whether concurrency improves task success, whether it is really faster, and which interaction mechanisms determine gain or failure.

The Agent state here consists of the main track, workspace, and sub-Agent return results; observations include warehouse, tool output, and sub-Agent messages; actions include forking, continuing, or terminating sub-Agents, and editing, testing, and committing code. The paper compares concurrent and serial execution under the same Agent, the same model, the same prompt, the same environment and time budget, and tries to converge the differences into the scheduling method.

## Method

The experiment covers SWE-bench Verified 100 questions, RepoZero 150 questions, NL2Repo 26 questions, ProgramBench 50 questions and LoopsBench 28 questions, a total of 354 different tasks. The system combinations are Codex+GPT-5.4, Claude Code+Claude Opus 5, Kimi Code+Kimi K3; each combination runs concurrent and serial modes respectively, so there are 2,124 executions in total.

Serial mode requires one child agent to complete before starting the next; concurrent mode allows dynamic overlap. The author unifies the model, inference gear, prompts, task environment and time budget, and records success, test pass rate, token, running time and execution trajectory. 1,062 concurrent trajectories were manually reviewed, 650 attributable cases were retained, and 804 pattern instances were annotated, resulting in 4 categories, 13 subcategories, and 28 specific patterns.

Unlike work that only compares static multi-agent topologies, this study observes the dynamic concurrency of agents themselves deciding when to fork and integrate child agents; and unlike a pure success rate evaluation, it simultaneously examines cost, wall clock time, and trajectory-level causal clues.

## Experiments and evidence

**Overall effect:** On SWE-bench Verified, the number of successful tasks in Codex increased from 71 serial to 71 concurrently, which is actually the same; Claude dropped from 83 to 59, and Kimi dropped from 77 to 59. The Holm-corrected McNemar test showed that Claude's decrease p=5.08348×10⁻⁷, Kimi's decrease p=0.0132176; Codex's change p=0.229481, not significant.

**Differences in task structure:** Across the three Agents, the average test pass rate changes of the long-range benchmark are RepoZero −8.29 percentage points, NL2Repo −0.04 percentage points, ProgramBench +1.24 percentage points, and LoopsBench +4.31 percentage points. Among the task success numbers of LoopsBench, Codex is 21.4→25, Claude is 7.1→21.4, and Kimi is 25→25; the direction indicates that splittable loops are more suitable for concurrency, but cannot be extrapolated to general warehouse tasks.

**Cost and speed:** Concurrent mode consumes 1.41~3.31 times tokens than serial mode; 14 of the 15 Agent-baseline combinations have increased running time. For the 366 tasks completed in both modes, concurrency was faster on only 102 tasks, accounting for 27.9%; if it was required to be at least 20% faster and save 60 seconds, it was only faster on 46 tasks, accounting for 12.6%. The total cost of the experiment at the public API list price was $20,780.02.

**Failure mechanism:** Track review shows that common problems include multiple sub-agents modifying the same file resulting in overwriting, subsequent tasks starting before the predecessor results are ready, the main agent mistaking local completion for global completion, sub-agent output not being verified or merged, and too much parallel exploration crowding out final testing and repair time. These patterns explain why token growth does not automatically translate into success rates or wall clock returns.

## Limitations and risks

**Explicit limitations from the author:** The results only cover 3 cutting-edge Agents and 5 coding benchmarks; concurrent decisions are jointly generated by specific Harness and models and cannot directly represent other systems. Trajectory-level causal attribution relies on manual judgment. Although there are classification specifications, it may still be affected by the annotator's interpretation.

**Research inference:** Unified wall clock budget cannot completely equivalently control supplier queuing, caching and tool service fluctuations. The paper discloses the total cost, but does not break down the input, output, cache, and main/sub-Agent tokens enough to accurately recalculate the cost. The GitHub artifact repository is public but no license is detected, and the root directory lacks an easily discoverable aggregation script entry. The cost of reuse boundaries and replication operations is still high.

If the concurrency strategy directly enters the production coding link, it must also constrain shared writing, dependency graph, cancellation propagation and final ownership; otherwise "more agents" will turn logical errors into scheduling errors and amplify costs.

## Reproduction and application

The suitable reproduction sequence is: first recalculate the success, test, token and running time of each task from the public trace; then use a small number of self-owned tasks to compare the seriality and concurrency of the same Agent; and finally expand to pay for full-scale running. Scheduling events, workspace diffs, sub-agent return values, and final tests should be saved at the same time, not just the final answer.Engineering applications should not enable maximum concurrency by default. More reasonable gating conditions include: whether subtasks write to mutually independent files, whether there is a clear dependency graph, whether the merged results can be automatically verified, and whether the remaining wall clock time is sufficient for integration. For shared core files, serial debugging, or tasks that require unified design judgment, serial or controlled small concurrency is more robust.

## Related work

- **SWE-bench Verified:** Provides real warehouse repair tasks, which this paper uses as a common evaluation field for concurrency and serialization.
- **RepoZero:** emphasizes understanding and modifying the warehouse from scratch; in this paper, the average concurrency degradation is the most obvious, exposing shared context and coordination issues.
- **NL2Repo:** Build warehouse-level implementation from natural language; overall concurrency is close to flat.
- **ProgramBench:** Examines longer-range program synthesis; only small average gains are achieved with concurrency.
- **LoopsBench:** The task structure is easier to split, and the concurrency benefits in this paper are the most stable, forming a positive comparison of task parallelism.

## Research judgment

**Reading Priority:** High. It directly answers the most intuitively misguided question in current multi-agent coding systems: when to be concurrent and when to be serial.

**Confidence:** Medium to high. Controlled pairing, task coverage, statistical testing, trajectory annotation, and public artifacts collectively support the main conclusions; but commercial system fluctuations, manual attribution, and replication costs limit extrapolation.

**Applicable to:** Designers of coding agents, task orchestrators, workspace isolation, and multi-agent evaluation systems.

**Follow-up observations:** Whether licenses and aggregation scripts are supplemented; whether independent re-runs occur; whether dynamic concurrency gating based on write sets, dependency graphs and remaining budgets can be trained or implemented; whether the differences between different Agents come from model capabilities or Harness scheduling.
