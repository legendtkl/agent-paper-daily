## Conclusion summary

- Strong Agent ability does not mean stable time control. AgentTime's 222 tasks covered 18 benchmarks and target durations from 1.25 minutes to 72 hours, and the strongest system completed only 63% within the ±5% target window.
- The old system ends significantly early: Claude Fable 5.1's logarithmic slope of actual duration versus target duration is only 0.59; GPT-5.6 Sol is 0.71; GPT-6 Astra improves to 0.94, but there is still a systematic bias.
- Extending the running time does not steadily improve mission scores. The average scores for both the longest and shortest time limits ranged from 62% to 64%, with only 23% to 25% of tasks performing better under longer time limits.
- 196 tests of the open code passed, but the current warehouse still separates the early 235-question prediction experiment from the 222-question time control experiment in the paper; the complete reproduction entrance of the main experiment has not yet been made public.

## Research question and background

Real Agent services need to be completed on time: users may require "results after 10 minutes of work", and the orchestrator also needs to predict resources and deadlines. Existing evaluations usually only look at whether the task is completed or the token upper limit, and rarely measure whether the agent understands the target duration, whether it can proactively allocate time, and whether extending the running time really improves the results.

The paper divides time capabilities into three layers: predicting how long a task will take before execution; controlling the actual running time after receiving a clear time limit; and reviewing the real duration from the trajectory after execution. The Agent's status includes remaining time, task progress, and tool results; its actions include continuing work, reviewing, sleeping, or ending early.

## Method

AgentTime contains 222 tasks, 18 benchmarks, and target durations ranging from 1.25 minutes to 72 hours. Three new runs were performed for each task-agent combination. The main experiment compared Claude Fable 5.1+Claude Code, GPT-5.6 Sol+Codex and GPT-6 Astra+Codex, analyzing a total of 1,991 valid runs; Fable retained 659 times due to 7 rejections, and the other two systems 666 times each.

Time control is measured by the ratio of actual duration to target duration, ±5% on-time rate, and log-log regression slope; slope 1 indicates how many times the target duration has expanded, and the actual duration has also expanded year-on-year. The paper also does Harness exchange, trajectory behavior annotation, task score comparison, pre-prediction and post-review, and eliminates "just a timing problem of a certain CLI" from different angles.

Unlike fixed tokens or static timeout benchmarks, AgentTime treats time as an explicit variable that the Agent must observe and control; unlike pure latency measurement, it checks whether the Agent is actively working, repeatedly reviewing, or aligning deadlines through sleep.

## Experiments and evidence

**On-time rate and scale control:** Fable's ±5% on-time rate is 4%, the actual/target duration ratio is 2.86 [2.68, 2.99], and the slope is 0.59 [0.54, 0.64]; Sol is 39%, 1.77 [1.59, 1.95] and 0.71 respectively; Astra is respectively 63%, 1.18 [1.11, 1.25] and 0.94. The intervals are from Table 3 of the paper; Astra is close to linear control, but not a perfect timer.

**Responses within the same task:** After controlling for task differences, the slopes for Fable, Sol, and Astra are 0.24, 0.58, and 0.90, respectively. When the target duration is expanded by 16 times, the actual duration is only expanded by 1.9, 5.0, and 12.1 times on average, indicating that weaker systems will ignore longer budgets and stop early.

**Harness swap:** After swapping Codex and Claude Code on 98 tasks, the model gap still exists. The slope advantage of Astra over Fable is 0.71 in Claude Code and 0.69 in Codex; the impact of replacing Harness alone is about 0.14~0.16. The evidence supports that "model capability is one of the main reasons", but differences in supplier services cannot be ruled out.

**Time usage:** Among the 158 Astra trajectories that can be classified, 14 are dormant, accounting for 9%; 74 are repeatedly checked, accounting for 47%. Among the subset of Agent-type tasks completed on time, only 11/49 continued to perform substantive work in the last fifth of the period, 24 were mainly reviewed, and 14 were mainly dormant. Punctuality in itself therefore cannot be equated with efficient use of budget.

**Longer is not necessarily better:** The average task score under the shortest and longest target time limits is 62% to 64%; only 23% to 25% of the tasks have improved scores under the longer time limit. This result refutes the simple assumption that "just give more wall clock time and you will continue to get better."

**Prediction and Review:** In the ex-ante predictions of 222 tasks, Fable, Sol, and Astra overestimated the duration by 63%, 83%, and 66% respectively, with typical errors of 2.6, 3.8, and 2.9 times; the prediction medians of the three were all 15 minutes, while the actual medians were 13, 6, and 10 minutes respectively. Among the 23 retrospective tasks, the error was about 1.03 to 1.33 times when retaining the original timing cues; after clearing the timing cues, the errors expanded to 2.58, 5.38, and 2.62 times, indicating that retrospectives rely more on explicit time cues in the trajectory.

**Scale and Cost:**The study totaled 3,442 Agent attempts, approximately 1,700 Agent hours; the author estimates the model API to be approximately $86,000 and hardware approximately $7,000 at publicly available prices, for a total of approximately $93,000.

**This verification:** Fixed the commit `b3491bb635e35ee7e3adf3ca8b214dbd25de1b31` locally to the warehouse, and ran the test in an independent virtual environment. The results were 196 passed, 22 skipped, and it took 27.71 seconds. This only proves that the unit tests of the public analysis and tool code have passed, and is not equivalent to reproducing the main experiment 1,991 times.

## Limitations and risks

**Explicit limitations from the author:** Only one run is generated per task-duration setting; Only 3 Agents are compared, and from 2 model labs; Vendor load, service layer timing and Harness overhead affect wall clock time; Instruction wording is fixed with the highest inference gear. There are only 4 missions longer than 8.3 hours, and evidence for the 72-hour endpoint is particularly sparse.

**Research inference:** Three runs can alleviate randomness, but it is still insufficient for long-tail service fluctuations. The ±5% window for on-time performance is very strict for short missions, but can tolerate larger absolute errors for longer missions, and should be read together with the absolute minute error. The closed-source model and billing services involved in the paper will continue to change, and subsequent reruns may not be completely comparable to the current version.

The public data is under CC BY 4.0 and the code is under MIT; however, the Hugging Face track set requires consent to access conditions. The paper states that there are 8,911 trajectories in total, of which 1,991 runs of the main time-controlled experiment have only 777 complete trajectories. The warehouse README states that the currently public `forecasting/` still corresponds to the early 235-question version. The main experimental code of the paper will be added later. At this stage, all charts cannot be reconstructed with one click.

## Reproduction and applicationThe current lowest cost reproducible path is to run warehouse analytics tests, examine the publicly available structured data, and recalculate slopes, on-time performance, and confidence intervals. To reproduce Agent behavior, you need to lock model snapshots, Harness versions, regional and supplier service conditions, and save server time, tool time, and model call time to avoid misinterpreting queuing delays as Agent decisions.

The product side should not only provide "maximum running time". A more robust interface would include both soft deadlines, remaining budget observations, stage checkpoints, minimum effective work requirements, and early termination reasons. For asynchronous tasks, you can have the Agent report progress and remaining work periodically instead of sleeping to fill the time limit.

## Related work

- **METR long task evaluation:** Provides long-term tasks organized according to human completion time. AgentTime inherits its duration scale and adds explicit time control.
- **PostTrainBench:** Provides longer training tasks, but the samples in AgentTime that exceed 8.3 hours are mainly concentrated in a few tasks, which is still a weak area of ​​evidence.
- **RE-Bench:** focuses on machine learning research engineering capabilities; AgentTime uses its tasks to check whether extending time improves the score.
- **SWE-bench series:** Provides software repair tasks, constituting short to medium-duration time-controlled scenarios.
- **Agent runtime prediction:** Existing jobs often predict the cost or success rate. AgentTime further tests whether the Agent itself can predict, control and review wall clock time.

## Research judgment

**Reading Priority:** High. Time control is the basic capability of Agent orchestration, cost commitment and asynchronous delivery, but direct evaluation has been lacking for a long time.

**Confidence:** Medium to high. Task coverage, three runs, Harness exchange and multi-layer behavioral analysis support the main conclusions; single task-long run, closed source service fluctuations and incomplete main experiment code limit recurrence.

**Applicable objects:** Agent platform, scheduler, long-range evaluation, cost and service level target designers.

**Follow-up observations:** Whether the main experiment code is fully disclosed; whether the slope difference is reproduced in more models and the local inference system; how to distinguish between effective work, repeated reviews and waiting; whether time prediction errors can be incorporated into dynamic budgets and abort strategies.
