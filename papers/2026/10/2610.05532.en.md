## Conclusion summary

1. The paper proves that only letting the model determine "whether it should act" and calculate the consistency rate with human labels cannot reliably predict whether the Agent will ask first after actually getting the tool.
2. 156 synthetic scenarios, 10 models, and 7,800 main evaluations show that the simple keyword rule has a higher consistency rate than 8 models, but only changes decisions for 9 pairs of 48 pairs of key condition changes; the high consistency rate may come from wrong shortcuts.
3. If the same decision is asked in an equivalent way, the action rate of a single model will change by up to 52.5 percentage points. All models actually performed fewer queries in the Tool-Loop than in the True/False question; one model dropped from 47.5% to 4.2%.
4. When the rules are clearly written, the balanced accuracy of the three models reaches 97.3% to 100%. The problem is mainly the boundary identification and evaluation method under vague delegation, not the model's complete inability to comply with the rules.
5. The artifact is complete, but the scene is short and synthesized, with only 3 technical students for artificial reference. Tool-Loop changes multiple conditions at the same time, so the paper records the judgment-to-action gap but does not identify its causal source.

## Research question and background

Agents that can send emails, delete files, place orders, and change codes must choose between "execute directly" and "ask first." Existing evaluations often present a proposed action to the model, let the model choose whether to continue, and then rank it according to the consistency rate with human labels. This design implies two untested assumptions: high-scoring models truly focus on scope, risk, reversibility, and visibility; and the degree of caution in true-false questions will be transferred to tool execution.

This paper does not directly propose a new permission system, but tests whether this evaluation has construct validity. The core questions are: whether scores are sensitive to changes in critical conditions, whether equivalent prompts are stable, whether the model actually asks when it says it will ask in a real tool loop, and whether approval after asking translates into correct action.

## Method

**Four types of decisions.** Each scenario requires the model to choose between Act, Ask, Request, and Refuse, which represent direct action, requesting permission, supplementing missing information, and rejecting respectively. Most scenarios come in pairs, changing only whether they are within the scope of the request, whether they are high or low risk, whether they are revocable, or whether the results are visible to the outside world.

**Fourth level evaluation.** The main study allowed 10 models to run 156 scenarios 5 times each; the format study rewrote 48 paired scenarios into 5 equivalent expressions; Tool-Loop turned the same scenario into a task with 15 simulation tools and up to 3 rounds; Post-Approval uniformly replied "agree" to the 227 continuations after the inquiry, and then checked whether to act. Two blind reviewers performed a human correspondence audit on 200 tool calls.

**Explicit rule comparison.** The paper also uses synthetic policies, fact rewriting, and 17 τ-bench retail decision points to test whether the model can perform when the authorization rules are clearly written. This contrast distinguishes "unable to understand authorization" from "failure to form robust boundaries under vague conditions".

## Experiments and evidence

**Main experiment.** 10 models from 5 series, each model completed 156×5 judgments, a total of 7,800 times. The four-category consistency rate is 50.6% to 73.4%; always Act or always Ask can also reach 46.3%/47.7%. A three-line keyword rule written after the fact achieved 69.1% agreement on human majority labels, better than eight models, but only changed the decision as expected in 9 of 48 pairings.

**Conditional sensitivity.** When the proposed action no longer falls within the scope of the user's request, the action rate of each model decreases by 78.3 to 100 percentage points; irreversibility reduces the action rate by 16.7 to 63.3 percentage points, and increased risk causes it to decrease by 0 to 65.0 percentage points. The impact of external visibility is close to zero. Thirteen of the 24 pairs of risk versus reversibility in the sample simultaneously varied other factors, limiting causal interpretation.

**Format and Action Gap.** The five equivalent price formulas of 48 questions caused the maximum change in single-model action rate by 4.2 to 52.5 percentage points. All models in the Tool-Loop asked fewer true/false questions: Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite dropped from 47.5% to 4.2%, and Gemini 3.6 Flash dropped from 40.0% to 6.7%. For 120 pure permission questions, 117 tools were called after fixing "agreement", a ratio of 97.5%; the recovery rate for mixed permission and information requests was even lower.

**Manual audit.** Two blind reviewers agreed 97.0% on 200 calls, with Cohen’s κ of 0.687, and estimated that 93.9%/96.0% of calls corresponded to proposed actions. Strict parameter matching only accepts 600/1,011 calls, indicating that format differences will be misjudged as action errors; however, "consistent with the proposed action", "mission correct" and "safety" are still different attributes.

**Local artifact inspection.** Fixed commit `55cf3aea056d54cfcad7ac127831ec55e9fcf887` containing 156 scenarios, annotations, model decisions, Tool-Loops, manual audit tags and results reconstruction scripts. When the system environment is run directly, 31 tests pass, and 2 drawing reproduction tests fail due to lack of matplotlib; installation according to locked dependencies is aborted due to lack of Fortran compiler in SciPy 1.13.1 under Python 3.13. This result demonstrates that most of the non-drawing verification can be performed and does not represent a complete reproduction of the 7,800 model calls.

## Limitations and risks

**Explicit restrictions by the author.** 156 scenarios are short and synthetic and do not illustrate the frequency of such situations in reality or real user preferences. There are only 3 computer students with AI experience in the manual reference. Krippendorff’s alpha is 0.543 for Act/non-Act, and 0.437 for the four categories; 7 scenes do not have most labels, and there are very few Request and Refuse samples.

**Tool-Loop restrictions.** There are only 48 scenarios, and 29 can achieve exact parameter matching; the tool has no persistent state, and two models have not completed all runs. Compared with true-false questions, Tool-Loop also hides the proposed action, changes the output format, and omits the permission prompt. Therefore, it can only prove that the gap exists, but cannot determine which change is caused.

**Research inference.** The paper's evaluation split of "when to ask first" is valuable, but cannot be directly translated into production permission strategies. If an organization regards the majority label of small-scale students as the normative truth value, it may solidify the risk preference of a specific group into system behavior; a more prudent approach is to write clear strategies, reversibility, and scope of influence as runtime constraints, and leave vague boundaries to users for confirmation.

## Reproduction and application

The repository provides scenarios, YAML/JSON schemas, main experiment decisions, derived results, validation scripts, and SHA256 manifests to check most main conclusions without re-invoking the model. The complete model re-run still needs to correspond to the commercial model, the same inference level and the provider version.

For engineering applications, the most worthy of adoption are five separate reports: consistency rate and excessive/insufficient intervention to human reference, pairing condition responsiveness, equivalent price formula fluctuations, interrogation behavior when the tool is executed, and action correspondence confirmed by manual sampling. For real agents, explicit permission policies are almost correctly implemented by the model, indicating that writing boundaries into Harness and tool access control is more reliable than relying on the model to guess on its own.
