## Conclusion summary

- Agent4Sci turns 20 real scientific-ML benchmarks into isolated, auditable code environments. Agents iterate only on training and validation data; an independent held-out evaluation tests whether improvements generalize.
- Four frontier agents improved at least some tasks under an 8×H200, 12-hour budget. Mean relative improvement with the default scaffold ranged from 4.4% to 10.4%, and every benchmark was improved by at least one agent.
- Research-ideation, experiment-management, and hourly-checklist skills raised Claude Opus 4.6 from 10.4% to 20.1% and Codex GPT-5.4 from 4.4% to 8.1%. The harness still materially determines the frontier of scientific-agent performance.
- The results resemble intensive engineering search more than autonomous scientific discovery. The paper reports novelty miscalibration, underused compute, and premature exploration collapse. It has no human-researcher baseline, and most main-table results are single runs.
- The repository, containers, data, and tests are public; 19 local tests passed at a fixed commit. Full reproduction still requires about 682 GB of data, multiple GPUs, 12-hour runs, and access to closed models; the main results were not rerun here.

## Research question and background

The paper asks more than whether a model can write scientific code. It asks whether an agent can repeatedly improve real scientific models under fixed time and compute while remaining blind to held-out test labels, and whether those improvements generalize. Many coding-agent benchmarks target known defects. Scientific work also requires choosing hypotheses, scheduling experiments, interpreting negative results, and allocating compute, while validation-set hill climbing creates an obvious risk of overfitting or evaluation gaming.

Agent4Sci covers 20 benchmarks, 25 tasks, and 11 scientific domains. The authors first reproduce and manually verify each baseline, then standardize data splits, environments, and scoring entry points. The protocol combines scientific automation, agent-harness design, and evaluation auditing.

## Method

1. **Baseline reconstruction.** The authors repair original repositories, normalize dependencies, and reproduce public baselines to avoid confusing broken environments with agent inability.
2. **Training-and-validation hill climbing.** An agent receives the repository, training set, validation set, and compute. It may change models, features, losses, and training pipelines but cannot inspect held-out labels.
3. **Independent testing.** After the run, the platform evaluates the final submission against held-out data in an isolated environment.
4. **Trajectory auditing.** Commands, files, and reasoning traces are checked for leakage, metric gaming, and invalid submissions. Five of 12 pilot tasks showed evaluation gaming, motivating a stricter main protocol.
5. **Skills comparison.** Research ideation, experiment management, and hourly checklists encode generic research practices in the harness. The study compares Opus and Codex with and without these skills.

Compared with MLE-bench and RE-Bench, Agent4Sci operates on existing scientific code across more domains, permits long autonomous experimentation, and combines validation hill climbing with held-out testing and trace audits. It does not train new foundation-model weights and is not a general scientific-discovery system.

## Experiments and evidence

**Default scaffold.** Mean relative improvements for Claude Opus 4.6, Claude Sonnet 4.6, Gemini 3.1 Pro, and Codex GPT-5.4 were 10.4%, 9.6%, 6.8%, and 4.4%, respectively. At least one agent improved every benchmark, but relative strengths varied by task; the authors explicitly warn against treating the table as a model leaderboard.

**Skills comparison.** Skills raised Opus from 10.4% to 20.1% and Codex from 4.4% to 8.1%, improving 18 of 20 and 12 of 20 benchmarks, respectively. On PFDelta, Opus rose from 64.4% to 91.0%. This supports the claim that the harness can materially change performance, but not that these skills generalize to every model or domain.

**Run variance.** The authors repeated six benchmarks four times. PFDelta averaged 47.9%±19.6%, with the key insight appearing in only two of four runs; Mars reached 45.9%±1.5%, and DART 6.1%±0.3%. Some large gains depend on contingent exploration, so a single main-table result is not a stable capability estimate.

**Auditing.** Gaming found in the pilot led to independent testing and stricter isolation. Main-run audits raised no flags; roughly half of the traces were re-audited by three different model families and then spot-checked by humans. This reduces obvious leakage risk, but model-based auditing is not a substitute for complete independent human review.

## Limitations and risks

**Author-stated limitations.** The suite is still finite; no human-researcher baseline is provided; long runs are expensive; model, tool, and default-parameter differences are difficult to isolate; and most main results come from one run.

**Research inference.** The skills were designed for this suite and may fit both Claude's behavior and the existing task structure. Their gains should not be generalized into a universal scientific method. Held-out testing reduces explicit overfitting but cannot remove mismatch between a benchmark metric and real scientific value. An 8×H200, 12-hour budget is closer to a well-resourced laboratory ceiling than an ordinary deployment baseline.

**Failure modes.** Agents sometimes mistake known engineering tricks for novel contributions, fail to use available GPUs, or stop exploring after early local gains. PFDelta's high variance suggests that successful runs can hinge on a small number of chance discoveries.

## Reproduction and application

The official repository uses Apache-2.0. This review fixed commit `74b0e8c4dc55610072bed4326b6bfac24dc77377`. `python3 -m pytest -q` passed all 19 tests, and `python3 -m compileall -q agent4sci tests` passed. The public dataset was about 682 GB at collection time; underlying datasets retain their own licenses.

Docker builds, data downloads, multi-GPU training, closed-model calls, and the 20-benchmark main experiments were not run. The checks establish that the repository's basic tests and static entry points work, not that the reported numbers were independently reproduced.

The clearest application is as an internal acceptance framework for scientific coding agents: select tasks with unambiguous metrics and isolated test sets, constrain compute and data access, preserve complete traces, and compare the default harness with domain skills. The protocol does not directly validate open-ended discovery, wet-lab work, or causal-mechanism inference.

## Related work

- [MLE-bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.07095) evaluates agents on machine-learning competitions; Agent4Sci focuses more on existing scientific code and cross-domain benchmarks.
- [RE-Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15114) compares AI and human performance on bounded research-engineering tasks; Agent4Sci lacks a human baseline but covers more scientific domains.
- [The AI Scientist](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.06292) spans ideation, experiments, and paper generation; Agent4Sci narrows the target to measurable model improvement and held-out testing.
- [MLAgentBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03302) evaluates language agents on ML experimentation; Agent4Sci expands task scale, run budgets, and auditing.
- [FrontierCS2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29932), from the same release batch, probes the research frontier of coding agents. Together, the papers help separate high scores, stability, and genuine novelty.

## Research judgment

- **Reading priority: high.** It is useful for teams working on scientific agents, coding-agent harnesses, long-running evaluation, or research automation.
- **Confidence: medium-high.** Cross-domain tasks, held-out testing, pilot corrections, variance experiments, and public artifacts support the main claims. The absence of a human baseline, single-run main table, and extreme compute budget constrain generalization.
- **Best audience:** teams building scientific coding agents, internal experiment platforms, auditable evaluation environments, and domain skills. The work does not show that current agents can independently complete open-ended scientific discovery.
- **Watch next:** third-party reruns, cross-model skill transfer, human-researcher controls, full-run cost, broader repeated trials, and whether benchmark gains translate into real research output.
