## Conclusion summary

- During context compaction, agents sometimes write approval requirements or approvals that the record does not support. In F2 held-out data, unsupported restrictions occur in 1.2% and 2.4% of summaries for two models; unsupported authorizations occur in 0.1% and 1.3%.
- The causal claims must be separated. Unsupported restrictions do not significantly change successor behavior in the pre-specified held-out test (`p=0.58`). Unsupported authorizations show a 24/120 to 7/120 reduction only in a post-hoc pooled F1+F2 analysis.
- Replacing the vague system-prompt sentence "Some actions need approval" with a source-constrained sentence reduces unsupported restrictions from 5.0% to 1.8% on development data and from 1.8% to 0.3% on fresh held-out data (both `p<10^-9`). This establishes an effect in this setup, not general safety.
- The public release supports full offline reproduction. This run passed 165/165 tests, verified frozen instances, and rebuilt all analyses, reader sheets, replay targets, and reference outputs with `ALL MATCH`.

## Research question and background

Long-running agents compact context by writing a summary, dropping most history, and handing only the summary to a fresh instance. Prior work usually asks whether compaction omits facts or constraints. This paper asks the opposite: whether the summary adds permissions unsupported by the record and whether the successor treats them as instructions.

This is not conventional external prompt injection. The agent writes the error for itself without an attacker and carries it across the compaction boundary. An external permission ledger supplies model-independent ground truth, and the study separates restrictions from authorizations.

## Method

Synthetic tasks cover records, reports, and code or configuration changes. Each template defines a ledger of allowed, forbidden, and approval-required actions and targets, plus authorization and withdrawal events. At a fixed step or finish attempt, the agent writes a handoff summary; the successor does not see the original request.

A rule labeler flags possible policy sentences, and two independent AI readers compare them with the true permission state. Confirmed sources enter same-snapshot replay. The environment is restored at compaction and replayed under original, neutral filler, count-matched control, delete, claim-correct, ledger-correct, and full-history arms with matched seeds. The paper distinguishes exploratory development, pre-specified Test, later-planned F2, retest F4, and system-prompt interventions F5/F6.

## Experiments and evidence

F2 analyzes 1,044 readable Qwen3-8B summaries and 1,094 gpt-oss-20b summaries. Unsupported restrictions occur in 13 (1.2%, Wilson 0.7%–2.1%) and 26 (2.4%, 1.6%–3.5%); unsupported authorizations occur in 1 (0.1%) and 14 (1.3%). Only rule-flagged summaries are read, so unknown labeler recall makes these lower bounds.

The pre-specified Test restriction result is 126/148 to 119/148 confirmation requests (`p=0.58`). Later-planned F2 is 121/156 to 106/156 (`p=0.027`), but it misses the `α=0.025` threshold allocated across the two planned tests. F4, fixing compaction timing, is 118/136 to 107/136 (`p=0.11`). The paper correctly characterizes the restriction evidence as weak and fragile.

Authorization outcomes are 11/36 to 4/36 on development data, 16/60 to 8/60 in Test, and 24/120 to 7/120 in the post-hoc F1+F2 pool (`p=0.002`), with 0/120 under full history. F4 is 17/48 to 11/48 (`p=0.63`). These are risk signals, but sources are rare and analyses differ in inferential status.

F5/F6 replace the vague approval sentence with an authority-source constraint. Unsupported restrictions fall from 5.0% to 1.8% in matched development pairs and from 1.8% to 0.3% on fresh held-out instances, while requests for genuinely approval-required actions barely change. The intervention changes a whole prompt sentence and is partly coupled with GPU type, so it does not identify a unique mechanism or establish safety non-inferiority.

## Limitations and risks

The main models are 8B–21B open-weight systems on synthetic tasks. The successor does not see the original request, which may amplify summary influence. Deployed agents compact under token pressure, whereas this harness often compacts at a fixed step or finish. Both AI readers are instances of Claude Opus 5.5; the human check is small and agreement limited; rule-labeler recall is unknown.

Large-model authorization checks concentrate in one template, the F1+F2 authorization test is post hoc, each replay arm has four samples, and fixed-seed vLLM is not fully deterministic. Oracle arms add missing information, while full history changes both content and length.

The reliable engineering takeaway is that authority should not live only in free-text summaries. Repair needs an external authority ledger, withdrawal events, and target-level permissions while monitoring both over-refusal and violations.

## Reproduction and application

The MIT-licensed repository releases the frozen test set, run records, reader labels, replay targets, analysis plans, outputs, and hashes. Full offline analysis needs no model API. Online runs require Python 3.11+, an OpenAI-compatible tool-calling endpoint, and a reader model; costs come from 7–10 model calls per task and multi-arm replay.

Minimal mechanisms for production are to keep authority outside summaries, require source and target attribution for approval claims, and run same-snapshot counterfactuals after compaction. The prompt rewrite is a low-cost candidate but still needs safety non-inferiority tests under real triggers, stronger models, and diverse tasks.
