## Conclusion summary

- Agora represents results, hypotheses, insights, verifications, and reports as immutable commits in a Git-backed DAG. Parent edges identify what a claim builds on, while derived views expose the frontier, verification status, and neglected branches.
- Thirteen Claude Code/Codex worker sessions posted 1,703 contributions over nearly 12 days and improved a frozen 119.6M-parameter hybrid from 3.3923 to 1.8990 bits per byte. The system recorded 165 internal independent reproductions with no reported failure.
- Roughly 98% of the total improvement arrived in the first 18 scored contributions. The next 1,106 scored contributions improved about 0.03 bpb, showing that shared state did not prevent concentration or parallel rediscovery.
- The paper correctly separates auditable records from causal evidence: it did not compare Agora against isolated workers, a flat log, or a central planner under matched budgets, and the community escaped its first basin only after a human deployed diversity views.
- The coordination mechanism is useful, but public reproducibility is limited because the implementation, complete DAG, containers, and frozen aggregation code are not linked.

## Research question and background

Autonomous research sessions can run experiments but often leave failures, negative results, and verification state inside temporary transcripts or worktrees. Parallelizing those sessions increases search capacity while also increasing duplicated work, early convergence, and lost provenance. Existing multi-agent systems usually coordinate a team within one task; Agora targets durable state across asynchronous sessions without a central planner.

The agents observe a shared DAG, leaderboards, branch landscapes, and verification status. Their actions are selecting a parent commit, changing code, running the evaluator, and publishing a result, hypothesis, insight, or verification. This is directly a multi-agent coordination and long-term memory problem.

## Method

Each contribution is a Git commit tagged as a result, insight, hypothesis, verification, or report. Records carry parent hashes, structured descriptions, metrics, prediction ranges, and artifact references. Git provides content-addressed provenance; SQLite, an HTTP API, a CLI, and a Next.js UI provide queries and derived views. Quality is intended to come from downstream use and independent verification rather than votes.

Derived indexes expose current leaders, unverified claims, and sparse branches. Clustering, diversity summaries, and diversity-aware UCB distribute attention. Workers share no conversation, filesystem, role graph, or task assignment; each container reads the project brief and Agora state, chooses a parent, evaluates one change, and publishes a reproducible commit.

The experiment forbids target-model training and training-data access. Forward predictions from 141 open-weight donors are compressed into a frozen 119.6M attention-SSM hybrid. The final recipe builds a low-rank transition operator from donor next-token statistics, installs it in the embedding and output head, and adds short-range signals through sparse deterministic attention, FFN, and SSM edits.

## Experiments and evidence

The primary run lasted 11 days and 19 hours. Thirteen accounts used Claude Opus 4.7/Claude Code and GPT-5.5/Codex on five A100 and eight H100 nodes, each with an 80 GB GPU. A fixed evaluator scored 200 non-overlapping 512-token FineWeb-Edu segments. Same-hardware runs were bit-identical; A100/H100 differences reached 1.3×10^-3 bpb.

The 1,703 records included 1,124 scored results, 284 insights, 203 hypotheses, 165 verifications, and one report. The winner had a 145-commit ancestry spanning 15 accounts, with 115 of 144 parent edges crossing account boundaries. The 165 verifications covered 95 targets; 40 ancestors of the winner were reproduced.

The score improved from 3.3923 to 1.899044 bpb. Relative to a conventionally trained GPT-2 124M at roughly 1.0 bpb, the authors describe this as closing 62% of the gap. Every method choice used the same 200-text development evaluator, so the defensible result is approximately 3.39 to 1.90, not the final decimal places. Table 4 explicitly states that adjacent milestones are search stages, not controlled ablations.

Coordination evidence also shows failure modes. The first 18 scored records delivered about 98% of the improvement; the next 1,106 yielded about 0.03 bpb. Among 696 cross-account equal-score pairs, 63% appeared within one hour and 80% within six hours. A human deployed clustering and diversity-aware UCB on day six; the first sub-1.90 result followed the next day. This supports feasibility, not causal superiority.

## Limitations and risks

The authors acknowledge the absence of matched comparisons against isolation, a flat log, or central planning; one hand-designed task; a mid-run human intervention; persistent duplicated work; and a graph dominated by a narrow spine.

The 165 verifications came from agents inside the same system. Zero failures may partly reflect a deterministic evaluator and target-selection bias, so they are not external replications. Contribution nodes are not independent statistical samples. Shared state can also amplify a wrong consensus, contaminate downstream branches, or retain sensitive artifacts indefinitely in Git.

Public artifacts are the main gap. The appendix specifies the ideal package—server, CLI, UI, evaluator, harness, containers, hardware identities, complete DAG, event logs, and frozen aggregation code—but no public entry point is supplied.

## Reproduction and application

Reproduction requires the project brief, contribution schema, tag semantics, scoring policy, exact Agora revisions, model and sampling settings, container/driver/hardware identities, the complete DAG, event-level failures and resource usage, and code that regenerates all reported analyses. The public paper does not currently provide that package.

The most transferable practice is to persist negative results, parent dependencies, independent verification, and executable artifacts across agent sessions. A deployment should first run a matched four-arm evaluation—isolated workers, flat log, central planner, and DAG—with identical agents, compute, and wall-clock budgets.

## Related work

- [AutoResearch](https://github.com/karpathy/autoresearch): a single unattended coding-agent research loop; Agora targets state sharing across many sessions.
- [The AI Scientist](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.06292): an end-to-end research pipeline; Agora supplies an institutional state layer rather than a fixed research workflow.
- [Agent Laboratory](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.04227): staged multi-agent research; Agora leaves branch and task selection decentralized.
- [MLE-bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.07095): evaluates machine-learning engineering agents; Agora focuses on persistent community state and verification lineage.
