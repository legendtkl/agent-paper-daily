## Conclusion summary

- ExplorationBench uses two executable “alien worlds” whose rules deliberately contradict familiar semantics to test whether agents choose experiments, accumulate evidence, infer rules, and transfer them to unseen tasks rather than merely recall pretrained knowledge.
- AlienCode contains 31 discovery targets and 70 held-out tasks; AlienLogic contains 24 modified rules and 70 held-out theorems. An interpreter and a proof checker provide exact grading without an LLM judge.
- Ten systems run three independent trajectories per sandbox. The highest AlienCode Best@3 rises from at most 15.7% before exploration to 87.6%, while matched extra turns without environment feedback remain at 0.5%–11.0%. Autonomous probes beat hindsight replay for 9 of 10 systems.
- Discovering a rule does not guarantee using it: tasks are solved only 70.9% of the time when all required rules are correctly reported. Same-system AlienCode trajectories differ by up to 72.8 points, and 6/30 finish at least three points below an earlier milestone.
- The study covers two deterministic synthetic worlds and four exploration rounds. Three trajectories per system are too few for precise reliability estimates, and private tasks prevent full third-party reproduction. It measures rule acquisition in verifiable synthetic environments, not real scientific discovery.

## Research question and background

Research and engineering agents must decide which evidence to collect, revise hypotheses after experiments, and apply new rules to unseen problems. Static benchmarks cannot easily exclude pretrained recall, while genuinely open scientific claims lack cheap and immediate ground truth. The paper addresses this tension by making the world unfamiliar to the model but fully executable to the evaluator.

The primary category is `EV` because the main contribution is an evaluation protocol and benchmark. Online probe selection motivates `RP`; scientific investigation motivates `RA`.

## Method

AlienCode is a small programming language with familiar syntax and hidden semantics—for example, integer literals are XORed with 27, while operators and indexing rules are altered. AlienLogic patches natural-deduction rules and includes both provable and unprovable held-out theorems. Each world supplies a flawed manual and fixed seed examples, so ordinary semantic priors mislead rather than solve the task.

Each trajectory explores for four continuous rounds. The model submits program probes in AlienCode and proof probes in AlienLogic. After each round, the conversation is copied, tools are disabled, and the system separately reports inferred rules and answers 70 held-out questions. The evaluation copy is discarded, so its answers cannot become evidence for later rounds.

The study compares autonomous exploration, hindsight replay, fixed probes, extra turns without tools, direct answering, and open-book conditions. Best@3 selects the highest final endpoint among three independent trajectories, while Mean@3, the worst trajectory, and all endpoints are also reported.

## Experiments and evidence

Ten frontier systems each run three trajectories in each sandbox. Every round allows at most 12 tool calls; AlienCode caps a run at 48 calls and AlienLogic at 48 proofs. Each milestone answers every task three times, separating answer noise from trajectory variation.

No AlienCode trajectory exceeds 15.7% before exploration. After four autonomous rounds, the best Best@3 reaches 87.6%, and seven of ten systems exceed 60%. Extra model turns without feedback end at 0.5%–11.0%. Autonomous probes outperform replay of the same system's best probe sequence for nine of ten systems, showing that history-conditioned experiment selection matters.

Two keystone offset rules affect 51 of 70 AlienCode tasks. Thirteen of 15 trajectories ending at or above 50% report both correctly. Of 13 jumps of at least 30 points between adjacent milestones, 12 coincide with newly reporting R15 and 11 with R14. Correctly reported rule count correlates with final accuracy at r=0.85, but this is correlational evidence.

Across 647 task–trajectory pairs where all required rules are reported correctly, only 70.9% of tasks are solved. When at least one required rule is reported incorrectly, 34.3% still pass, making natural-language rule reports a lossy readout. Repeated-answer standard deviation is at most 4.7 points in AlienCode and 2.9 in AlienLogic, while same-system trajectory endpoints vary by as much as 72.8 and 21.0 points. Six of 30 AlienCode and three of 30 AlienLogic trajectories end at least three points below an earlier milestone.

Compute is not fully matched. AlienCode trajectories use nearly all tool calls, but Best@3 exploration tokens range from 0.34M to 11.59M. Vendor-specific maximum reasoning settings are not a common compute scale, so the leaderboard should not be interpreted as a compute-matched model comparison.

## Limitations and risks

The authors explicitly limit the claim to two deterministic, executable, four-round worlds. Real discovery includes noisy and incomplete observations, expensive or irreversible experiments, open-ended hypotheses, and much longer horizons. Three trajectories and three answers per task reveal variability but do not precisely estimate either distribution.

The private tasks reduce contamination while centralizing reproduction with the authors. Public materials are insufficient to independently audit coverage, hidden rules, interpreters, proof checkers, or the leaderboard. The project page currently reports a top score of 89.0%, whereas the v1 paper reports 87.6%; this report consistently uses the v1 result.

Best@3 measures whether one strong path appears among three attempts, not typical reliability. Engineering evaluations should also report Mean@3, worst-case outcomes, trajectory variance, token and tool budgets, and the rate at which further exploration reverses progress.

## Reproduction and application

The paper provides construction principles, six conditions, the tool protocol, budgets, verifier formulas, per-system results, and case studies. The arXiv HTML is licensed CC BY 4.0. Missing public artifacts include the full tasks, private inputs, interpreters, proof checker, and executable scripts; no public code, dataset, or model repository was found.

The protocol is useful for research agents, unknown-API learning, and tool discovery: make rules conflict with existing priors, cap probe budgets, separate exploration history from tool-disabled held-out evaluation, and record both verbal beliefs and executable outcomes. Production systems should add belief checkpoints and regression tests so later exploration cannot silently overwrite correct discoveries.

## Related work

- [DiscoveryWorld](https://openreview.net/forum?id=cDYqckEt6d) evaluates scientific investigation in virtual worlds; ExplorationBench emphasizes prior-conflicting rules, exact verification, and post-exploration transfer.
- [MARS](https://openreview.net/forum?id=3qoQ6AolAz) studies situated induction in open worlds; this paper further separates probe ownership, feedback, and held-out application.
- [MLAgentBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03302) asks agents to improve ML experiments; this work uses synthetic worlds to control contamination and adds matched controls.
- [Voyager](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16291) accumulates skills in a sandbox; ExplorationBench keeps weights fixed and tests exploration within one context.
- [AEWM/EditAct](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28416) edits erroneous states before action; ExplorationBench exposes the failure mode where continued exploration overwrites correct beliefs.

## Research judgment

Priority: very high for agent evaluation, research agents, test-time adaptation, tool discovery, and reliability teams. Confidence: strong protocol definitions, executable grading, controls, trajectory-level reporting, and budget disclosure; limited by private tasks, few repeats, incomparable reasoning budgets, and project-page score drift.

Weighted dimensions (0–5): Agent relevance 5.0, contribution 5.0, evidence 4.8, reproducibility 2.1, freshness 5.0, and verifiable attention 3.0. The weighted score is 88.5; because the work directly changes the evaluation boundary for agent exploration, it is recorded as 90/100.

Watch next: task and verifier release, frozen leaderboard versions, confidence intervals from more trajectories, rankings under normalized token and tool budgets, and external validity across sessions, noisy evidence, and irreversible experiments.
