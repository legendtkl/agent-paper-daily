## Conclusion summary

- The paper separates a tool schema from an agent's actual action space. With tasks, native actions, and terminal states fixed, changing only the interface representation moves success from 0% to 97%, showing that fixed-schema benchmark scores include interface bias.
- Nine executable transformation operators produce up to 32 equivalent schemas. Eleven models, a controlled environment, τ²-bench, and BFCL show that merges and cross-call protocols are often more brittle than per-tool surface changes, but the hardest representation is model-dependent.
- Query-free schema probes do not reliably predict difficulty. Their mean Spearman correlation is 0.52–0.57; about 100 target queries raise it to 0.81 and 800 queries to 0.92.
- Training mostly repairs schemas it sees. Mixed-schema GRPO improves trained hard variants without lowering the untrained variants in this study; gains transfer to recombinations of seen transformations, not to genuinely new transformation types.
- The repository releases 2,748 tasks, 32 schema variants, real-benchmark adapters, result tables, and equivalence tests. Seven directly runnable test modules passed locally; model evaluations and training were not rerun.

## Research question and background

Tool-agent benchmarks commonly fix function names, descriptions, argument structures, and call protocols, then interpret performance on that interface as model capability. The same action can instead be exposed as one dispatcher or many small tools, flat or nested arguments, or a sequence such as begin/write/commit. If results move while task semantics and terminal state stay constant, rankings, training gains, and deployment choices partly reflect an arbitrary interface design.

The paper asks how large schema bias is, which failures equivalent schemas induce, how cheaply schema difficulty can be predicted, and whether training removes the bias or generalizes to unseen schemas. The controlled evaluation framework motivates `EV`; action representations and protocols motivate `TU`; systematic rejection, livelock, under-execution, and argument failures motivate `SS`.

## Method

The framework derives equivalent representations from a native schema with operators for merging and splitting tools, lowering or lifting arguments, renaming, stripping descriptions, reordering arguments, and three cross-call protocols: transactions, reference resolution, and schema discovery. Every variant includes the schema shown to the model, a decoder back to native actions, and an oracle encoder for gold native actions.

The equivalence gate checks both actions and states. Oracle-encoded gold calls must decode exactly; variant calls ultimately execute only native actions; invalid calls are rejected with recoverable errors. Roughly 50,000 reference-trace checks caught three implementation errors before evaluation and none after correction. The controlled environment contains 12 domains, 168 native operations, and 2,748 queries. Nine open models run on all 32 variants, while gpt-6-luna and GLM-5.3-Flash run on 13 representative variants. τ²-bench and BFCL retain their original environments and scorers behind a schema proxy.

## Experiments and evidence

Success spans 0% to 97% with tasks, native actions, and terminal states fixed. Fully merged tools reduce median success across nine open models by 19 percentage points; nested merging costs every model 19–73 points. Transaction and schema-discovery protocols collapse many earlier models toward zero, whereas reference resolution can preserve or improve performance for models that handle the extra resolver call.

Schema bias persists in τ²-bench airline and retail. In retail, full merging drives nearly every model toward zero. Qwen3.5-27B, the strongest tested model, loses under every alternative schema in both domains. Failures are classified as livelock, broken transaction handles, rejected-and-stuck calls, wrong class, under-execution, over-execution, and wrong arguments. Variants induce consistent failure types across models, showing that schema changes behavior, not only aggregate scores.

Query-free likelihood and compliance probes correlate with full variant rankings at 0.52 and 0.57. Thirty-six target queries reach 0.72, about 100 reach 0.81, and 800 reach 0.92. Rankings on synthetic tasks do not predict τ²-bench rankings, so samples must come from the target task distribution.

For Qwen3-4B, baseline success is 0.77 on the native schema, 0.36 on full merging, 0.28 on the transaction protocol, and 0.07 on schema discovery. Mixed-schema GRPO changes these by +0.04, +0.43, +0.34, and +0.68. Mixed SFT repairs hard trained variants but reduces several previously robust variants by 0.06–0.10. Native-only training does not repair unseen hard variants.

All 288 cells in the 9×32 model–variant matrix are complete and paired by episode. At temperature 0.7, three independent seeds for two models preserve the expected difficulty order, with standard deviations at most 0.008 and episode-bootstrap intervals reported. A full configuration takes about five GPU-minutes on eight RTX 5090 GPUs; the two SFT-then-GRPO runs each use 19,200 episodes and 200 updates, costing 24.2 and 24.5 GH200 GPU-hours.

## Limitations and risks

The authors explicitly show that difficulty depends jointly on model, schema, and task distribution; synthetic rankings do not directly transfer to real benchmarks. Training generalizes to combinations or neighbors of seen transformations, not to new transformation types. The real benchmarks mainly validate the existence of bias, while aggregate post-training gains on τ²-bench and BFCL remain small.

Our inference is that the paper's equivalence definition—same native actions and terminal states—does not cover call cost, latency, permission exposure, recovery experience, or intermediate side effects. Real APIs can therefore make two schemas operationally inequivalent. Open-ended objectives, partial success, and nondeterministic side effects also lack the programmatic gold calls available in the synthetic environment. Closed models cover only 13 representative variants and are not directly comparable to the full 32-variant open-model grid.

The repository's tests are executable modules rather than conventional pytest fixtures. Direct `pytest` collection mistakes the script parameter in `test_hard_rejects(comp)` for a fixture. Running the seven catalog-free modules through the documented `python -m tests.*` entrypoints succeeds. The eighth proxy test requires an explicit real catalog and was not run.

## Reproduction and application

The Python 3.10+ repository releases the transformation package, 2,748 queries, native tools, 51 spec files, 32 representative schemas, adapters for τ²-bench, BFCL, AutomationBench, and MCP-Atlas, result CSVs, and reproduction instructions. The repository uses CC BY 4.0; upstream benchmark assets retain their own licenses.

In a fresh temporary clone, seven modules passed: 78,180 ladder decodes; 14,564 first-wave operator replays; 24,384 second-wave replays; 140,148 spec episodes; 16,560 merge-structure checks; 10,992 hierarchy replays; and 2,280 SimEnv episodes. This validates the released transformations and gold replay path, not the reported model success rates, SFT, or GRPO results.

For practice, schema should become an evaluation variable. Test several state-equivalent representations, sample from the target workload rather than relying only on function-calling probes, and check both seen and unseen schemas after training. MCP, OpenAPI, and enterprise tool platforms must also measure permissions, cost, latency, and irreversible side effects rather than adopting terminal-state equivalence alone.

## Related work

- [τ-bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12045) evaluates tool-agent-user interaction with a fixed schema; this paper rewrites τ²-bench schemas while retaining tasks and scorers.
- [AppWorld](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.18901) provides a controllable app world and state-based scoring; this paper treats its interface as one action-space parameterization.
- [ToolSandbox](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.04682) evaluates stateful conversational tool use; this work adds executable, state-equivalent schema variation.
- [RoTBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.08326) studies corrupted tool descriptions, while this paper studies valid, reversible, functionally equivalent schemas.
- [ToolACE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.00920) trains function calling with granular tool data; this paper shows that mitigation generally remains tied to seen schema transformations.

## Research judgment

Reading priority: very high for agent evaluation, tool protocols, MCP/OpenAPI design, model training, and reliability teams. Confidence: high but awaiting external reruns. Controlled variables, the complete matrix, real benchmarks, seed and bootstrap checks, open artifacts, and local tests provide strong evidence; cross-task generalization of the training claims remains limited.

Weighted score: 93/100—agent relevance 5.0/5, contribution 4.8/5, evidence 4.8/5, reproducibility 4.6/5, freshness 5.0/5, and verifiable attention 1.2/5. The main deductions are sparse early community evidence, no independent model-training reproduction, and an equivalence definition that omits cost and side effects.

Watch for third-party replication of the 0%–97% range, stability on MCP and enterprise APIs, broader equivalence definitions that include cost and permissions, generalization to unseen operators, and statistically significant benchmark-rank reversals across schemas.
